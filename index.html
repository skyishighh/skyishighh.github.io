Deconstructions

How games I admire handle each skill, and what I take from them into my own work.

Level design & pacing

Limbo

Playdead, 2010

  • Only move, jump and grab, and no tutorial at all. The levels do every bit of the teaching.
  • Puzzles are built expecting you to die first. Quick respawns turn each death into a lesson.
  • Each puzzle uses very few pieces. The challenge is what you do with them, not finding them.
  • It often leads you to expect one thing, then pulls the rug out.
  • Where it slips: some deaths can't be avoided without already knowing they're coming.
What I take from it

One new rule at a time, with nothing else competing for attention. It's why Sky Hopper introduces one new obstacle per island.

Difficulty balancing

Candy Crush Saga

King, 2012

  • Hard spikes are deliberate. The infamous level 65 was hard because it was meant to end an update.
  • King judges levels with data, such as how long players take to give up, and reworks weak ones.
  • Bots play thousands of runs to estimate a level's difficulty before players ever see it.
  • Luck is kept in the mix on purpose, so less skilled players can still progress.
  • The criticism: some players feel certain levels are tuned to push them toward buying help.
What I take from it

Paint Flow already tests every level against bot playthroughs. Next I'd plan hard spikes with deliberate easy levels after them.

Environment mood

Inside

Playdead, 2016

  • No HUD, no text and no dialogue. The world tells the whole story.
  • A near-monochrome palette, where the boy's red shirt is one of the only bright colours.
  • The colour shifts from area to area, carrying the feeling of progress, emotion and tension.
  • Lighting is placed by hand rather than calculated automatically, so every scene is art-directed.
What I take from it

At Adro I gave each area its own colour mood, a cool crystal cave against a warm lava zone. In Seven Runners, red is reserved only for things that kill you.

Core mechanic design · Systems & economy

Royal Match

Dream Games, 2021

The core mechanic

  • A simple base: match three or more items of one colour, or blast them with power-ups, to reach each level's goal.
  • Better matches make stronger tools. Five in a line makes a Light Ball, five in an L or T makes TNT, and the harder a power-up is to make, the stronger it is.
  • Power-ups combine. A Light Ball with a Rocket turns every item of the most common colour into Rockets.
  • Generous and fast: power-ups come naturally from normal matches, and short animations keep the board readable.

The economy

  • One currency, coins, buys extra moves, boosters and lives. Easy to understand.
  • The Butler's Gift gives free boosters for winning levels in a row, and resets when you lose.
  • A monthly pass of 30 reward steps. The paid tier raises max lives from 5 to 8.
  • Offers appear at moments of friction: a hard level, or one move short of winning.
What I take from it

Playing well should feel rewarding before spending ever comes into it. Better matches earn stronger power-ups for free, and in Settle & Snap stars reward restraint the same way. A win-streak reward like the Butler's Gift is my next idea.

Let's make something players remember.
